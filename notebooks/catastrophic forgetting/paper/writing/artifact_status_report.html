Magnitude-law campaign · results summary

The Magnitude Law: Update Size Is the First-Order Predictor of Forgetting in PEFT

A controlled study of nine LoRA-family adapters on commonsense reasoning and math. The size of the weight update predicts forgetting; at matched update size the assessed methods land on the same curve as a tuned plain-LoRA baseline (the one apparent deviation traces to a calibration confound, §2), and they cost more to run or to set up — so LoRA + weight-decay is the best efficient operating point.

The adapters

Eight of the nine methods share the same form — a trainable low-rank update ΔW = (α/r)·B·A added alongside frozen base weights — and what distinguishes them is how that update is initialized or constrained. DoRA is the carve-out: it additionally re-parametrizes each weight as magnitude × direction and learns the magnitude — not the same form. For the SVD-initialized family the frozen matrix is the residual left after carving the initialization out of the pretrained W — not W itself — so training still starts exactly at the pretrained function.

Baselines

  • LoRA — the standard low-rank adapter; A random, B zero, so the update starts at zero and grows freely. No geometric constraint; its size is governed by learning rate, rank and training length.
  • LoRA + weight decay ours — plain LoRA trained with AdamW weight decay on the adapter factors A, B. The decay shrinks the L2 (Frobenius) norm of those trainable factors; since ΔW is built from B·A, this indirectly reduces the effective update's functional magnitude (our measured FΔ) — it does not penalize ΔW directly. It imposes no geometric structure (unlike the SVD-init methods below): the same rank-r LoRA parametrization plus one scalar knob that trades a little task fit for less forgetting, at essentially zero extra cost.
  • DoRA — splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction, applying the LoRA update to the direction and learning the magnitude separately; designed to close the gap to full fine-tuning, not specifically to reduce forgetting.

SVD-initialized

  • PiSSA — initializes the adapter from the base weight's principal (largest) singular directions; training begins on the most influential directions, so updates tend to be large and, in our study, forget the most.
  • MiLoRA — initializes from the minor (smallest) singular directions, on the premise that learning in the least-important directions leaves dominant pretrained knowledge undisturbed.

Constrained (data-free)

  • CLoRA — adds an orthogonality penalty during training that forces the update to annihilate a frozen subspace of k directions. Those directions are drawn at random and orthonormalized — no calibration data enters the loop (verified in our port, train_cs.py) — so its retention gain cannot come from targeting knowledge directions. Larger k constrains more random directions and shrinks the effective update: in our k-series FΔ falls monotonically 0.60 → 0.34 as k grows 128 → 2048 (retention 22.5 → 25.7) — k acts, in effect, as a magnitude knob, which is exactly how §1 finds it behaving.

Calibration-based

  • SC-LoRA — initializes in output directions that serve the fine-tuning task while avoiding those that carry the knowledge to preserve (two calibration sets; a scalar β sets the task-alignment vs preservation balance); the constraint is set once at init and (per its paper) erodes during training.
  • LoRA-Null — initializes A in the null space of calibration input activations, so the update initially does nothing (ΔW·x ≈ 0) on inputs drawn from the preserved-knowledge distribution.
  • CorDA++ — a context-oriented SVD weighted by calibration-activation covariance, plus dynamic per-layer rank; can run in a knowledge-preserving mode that adapts the least data-relevant directions.

The relationship is not an artifact of one setting or a few points — it replicates five separate ways:

  • Within every method. The law reproduces within every one of the seven assessed adapters (r −0.86 to −0.97).
  • Second architecture, commonsense — in full. Qwen2.5-7B, the same 7 adapters × 7 LRs: pooled r = −0.86, n = 49 — numerically indistinguishable from Llama-2's −0.86; the slope is steeper on Qwen's retention scale, ≈ −32 pp/decade, so the two fits are reported separately.
  • Second architecture, math — in full. Qwen2.5-7B on MetaMathQA, 6 method families × 7 LRs: pooled r = −0.71, slope −15.3 pp/decade — the same slope family as Llama-2 math (−13.9), Llama-2 commonsense (−14.8) and CLoRA's published table (−14.7) — on n = 42 non-degenerate cells (one LoRA+wd lr-1e-3 cell collapsed outright, FΔ 15.8 with 0 accuracy, and is excluded with disclosure). The pooled correlation is weaker than Llama's for a structural reason visible in the data: Qwen's base BBH is much higher (≈ 48 vs 33.1), so more of the sweep sits in the law's saturation region — including the entire LoRA+wd sweep (FΔ 0.04–0.12, retention pinned at 46.8–48.0 while GSM8K reaches 66.6), which replicates the efficient-operating-point result on the second model. Where a method's LR range does span the knee, the within-method law reappears (r −0.70 to −0.90 for CLoRA, SC-LoRA, MiLoRA, DoRA).
  • LR vs size. Decisively, the update size is a far better predictor of retention than the learning rate that produced it (R² 0.74 vs 0.32).
  • Broad retention battery. Nor is the law an artifact of which retention suite is scored. Every evaluation in the campaign records five retention benchmarks — the two headline suites plus a broad battery — and the law holds on each capability benchmark separately, on both task arms. The learning rate acts through the update size, not beside it. The curve saturates at the top, and the robust statistics are stronger than the straight line

Retention saturates near the base-model reference (26.0), so the left of the curve flattens (at heavy regularization runs can sit slightly above the reference). Accounting for that only strengthens the result: rank correlation Spearman ρ = −0.90; a saturating fit beats both a line and a parabola on cross-validation, with its knee at update size ≈ 0.37 — essentially LoRA+wd's operating point; and past the knee the true slope is ≈ −21 pp per decade of update size (vs −15 for the saturation-attenuated linear fit). The knee also marks where predictability changes: residual SD is ≈ 0.6 pp below it vs ≈ 3.5 pp above — large updates forget more and less predictably. The law survives controlling for adapter identity (partial r = −0.87) and a permutation test (p < 5×10⁻⁵).

Geometry analysis

Does the placement of the update itself protect knowledge, or only the size of the update? Measured from the trained weights directly, geometry acts through the size and allocation of the update — it sets where on the magnitude axis a method starts — rather than as an independent protection mechanism alongside it. Every adapter in §0 differs from plain LoRA in essentially one design choice — where in the weight space it places its update — and this section answers the question that choice implies (the question anyone adopting one of these methods is implicitly betting on) by reconstructing every update (ΔW = (α/r)·B·A, validated against the recorded values) and measuring where its energy went.

Three results:

  • (1) The advertised geometry is real. For the methods whose design lives in the base weights' SVD basis (MiLoRA, SC-LoRA, CorDA — and PiSSA on math), it survives training and is readable off the saved weights, which both verifies our ports and makes the design claims testable (LoRA-Null's input-side null space is defined against calibration activations, a basis this battery cannot see, so its design is not verifiable here).
  • (2) Placement adds no measurable retention benefit once update size is accounted for, among the methods on the law (rank remains a modest second-order effect).
  • (3) The two methods that fall below the size law do so because of their placement — it concentrates a large, mis-allocated update. This is measurable at matched hyperparameters: at every one of the seven swept LRs, SC-LoRA's update is 1.4–2.6× plain LoRA's, and CorDA's explodes (FΔ 515.8 at lr 1e-3 vs LoRA's 1.42); and the concentration statistically absorbs the deviation — adding ein_top to a size-only pooled model lifts R² from 0.63 to 0.75 and shrinks SC-LoRA's mean below-law residual from −5.5 to −1.1 pp.

How we measured it — from the saved weights alone

What we measured — the metrics from first principles. Two different counts appear here, so to be precise:

  • The objects. A Llama-2 run adapts 160 weight matrices (5 module types × 32 layers), and we analyze 320 saved adapter checkpoints (runs), each containing its own 160 trained updates.
  • The reference directions. The base weights are frozen and shared by every run, so we take one SVD per matrix — 160 SVDs, computed once — and read all 320 runs against the same directions. The SVD orders each base W's left singular vectors (directions in output space) and right singular vectors (directions in input space) by singular value — by how much of W's action flows through each. "Top-256" = the 256 most important of those directions; "bottom-256" = the 256 least important.
  • The neutral reference. Each subspace is 256 of 4096 dimensions, so an update with no directional preference would land ~6% of itself in each — that 0.06 is the neutral reference every number below is read against (exact for the 4096-dim sides; the MLP up/down projections have one 11008-dim side where the neutral value is ≈ 0.023, so the effective pooled neutral under the battery's energy weighting is ≈ 0.05).
  • The reconstruction. For each of the 320 runs, we reconstruct its 160 updates ΔW = (α/r)·B·A from the stored factors (no retraining) and ask, matrix by matrix, where the energy went.

"Energy" means squared Frobenius norm, ‖ΔW‖²F — the natural additive measure of "how much update" lives where. So "how similar are the update's directions to the base's?" is answered by projection: energy in a subspace is exactly the part of ΔW that survives projecting onto it — no angle heuristics.

  • e_top — ‖projection of ΔW onto the base's top-256 output directions‖²F / ‖ΔW‖²F: the fraction of the update's total energy landing in those directions (e_bot: same for the bottom-256).
  • ein_top / ein_bot — the same fractions on the input side, using the base's right (input) singular directions.
  • stable rank — ‖ΔW‖²F / σ²max(ΔW): the effective number of directions the update spreads over (1 = one direction dominates; larger = more spread out).
  • law residual — measured retention minus what the magnitude law predicts at that run's FΔ, in pp — positive means the run forgets less than its size alone predicts.

Why these are the right candidates.

  • Each metric directly tests a method's own design claim: MiLoRA claims minor-direction updates (→ e_bot / ein_bot), PiSSA principal directions (→ e_top), SC-LoRA input-principal alignment (→ ein_top), LoRA-Null an input-side null space — and the law residual then asks whether achieving that geometry buys any retention beyond update size.
  • Computable from the saved adapters alone, with no retraining; the ΔW reconstruction they rest on is validated against the spectral norms recorded at eval time (median relative error 0.005%, max 0.51% — bf16 rounding).
  • The full battery holds a few more fields — amp_top (MiLoRA's own Table-7 principal-block amplification, ‖Utopᵀ·ΔW·Vtop‖F/‖ΔW‖F), an entropy-based effective rank, and the raw Frobenius/spectral norms; the table shows the design-relevant subset, while the "adds nothing beyond size" stress test below controlled for the full battery.

Two levers, ranked

  • First lever — size. Retention tracks update size within every method (r −0.75 to −0.94 across the battery).
  • Second — rank. At matched update size, higher-rank (more spread-out) updates forget somewhat more (partial r ≈ −0.56, and −0.5 to −0.6 across alternative rank measures); this is largely the adapter rank r itself, so we state it as "size first, rank second," not a hidden axis.
  • Subspace alignment adds almost nothing beyond these: controlling for size and method, no alignment metric adds more than ~0.03 R² (amp_top ≤ 0.001, e_top ≤ 0.008; the worst cases, e_bot/ein_bot on the on-curve subset with rank controls, reach 0.02–0.03) — versus 0.63 for size alone, and where residual alignment effects exist at all they are small penalties, never protection. Among the on-curve adapters, magnitude rather than subspace alignment sets where a run lands.

The alignment metrics cleanly recover what each method was designed to do, and the design persists through three epochs of training. This is the constructive payoff: it identifies each method and explains, mechanistically, why the two methods with the strongest input-side alignment spikes (SC-LoRA, CorDA) are the two that fall below the magnitude law.

Control experiment — the one deviation traces to a calibration confound

SC-LoRA is the one method that falls measurably below the size law (−4.15 pp on the canonical fit). Its calibration draws the knowledge-to-preserve set from nq_open — a different distribution from the retention suites. An eval-matched calibration control (same recipe, same LRs, preserve-set drawn from the eval distribution) roughly halves FΔ at each learning rate and puts SC-LoRA back on — slightly above — the law: residuals +0.05 / +2.7 / +1.8 pp at lr 5e-5/1e-4/3e-4, versus +0.9 / −2.8 / −6.3 in the original arm, at unchanged adaptation (CS 80.1 ↔ 80.1 at 5e-5). The eval-matched condition is idealized — the nq_open arm remains the realistic deployment condition — but it shows the below-law deviation is a property of the calibration distribution feeding the placement, not of subspace placement as a mechanism: at matched update size, every assessed adapter lands on the same curve.

Boundary condition — high-rank CLoRA on commonsense

What they claim: in CLoRA's published results, its k1024/k2048 configurations reach 82.6/83.7 accuracy at BBH 36.5/38.7 — above our LoRA+wd, and their k2048 even exceeds the base model out-of-domain.

What replicates: the retention half of their high-k claim, riding on the smaller update exactly as the law predicts — in our pipeline that configuration retains 25.7 with BBH 34.1 above base.

What doesn't: the accuracy half, in our hands. In our pipeline high-k CLoRA lands on our law but below LoRA+wd on both axes (k1024: CS 79.9, retention 24.8 at FΔ 0.46; k2048's accuracy collapses to 65.4 — and is seed-fragile in the 3-seed matrix). A commensurability note: their 82.6/83.7 are their own harness (not directly comparable to our 81.6 — the same recipe gap that shows up on math, §4).

Why it stays a boundary rather than a refutation: high-k CLoRA sits on the magnitude law — it achieves that retention by shrinking the update (their own Table 4 shows k2048 at the smallest FΔ in their study). So the defensible statement is narrow: at high rank, CLoRA's null-space constraint buys adaptation-efficiency per unit of update that pure size control does not — while the magnitude law still governs both.

  • SVD-init (MiLoRA, PiSSA) — factor all 160 base matrices in a few minutes — negligible.

  • CLoRA — its cost is memory, and it is additional to the shared ~55 GB footprint: a frozen regularization block of +0.42 GB at k=128 rising to +6.7 GB at k=2048 (at the default k=512, +1.7 GB — already 8× the trainable LoRA weights) for no added capacity.
  • LoRA and LoRA+wd — pay none of these — zero init, no extra memory, plain-LoRA wall-clock.

Forgetting, measured the field's own way

As an independent check we implemented MiLoRA’s own forgetting metric — the cross-entropy of the fine-tuned model against the base model on WikiText-103 (following Kalajdzievski, 2024) — and it reproduces MiLoRA’s published numbers and independently confirms the magnitude law on a metric we did not design. The models scored here are the math-adapted models of §4 — Llama-2-7B fine-tuned on MetaMathQA — evaluated on text that is neutral to both the adaptation task and the retention suites.

How the metric is computed:

  • Corpus and scoring. WikiText-103's test set — neutral text none of these models was ever fine-tuned on — is concatenated and chunked into non-overlapping 1024-token blocks; we score 40 blocks × 1,023 next-token positions each = 40,920 scored positions per adapter.
  • Two distributions per position. At every position, both models predict a full next-token probability distribution: the frozen base model (obtained by disabling the adapter on the same wrapped model, so it is exactly the pretrained weights the update was added to) and the fine-tuned model.
  • The metric. The soft cross-entropy between the two distributions, CEt = −Σv pbase(v | context) · log pft(v | context), averaged over all positions. That is ordinary next-token training loss with the hard target label replaced by the base model's distribution — so it reads directly as "how far has the model's next-token behavior drifted from base on neutral text," not as hard-label perplexity. The definition follows Kalajdzievski (2024), and it is precisely the metric MiLoRA's paper reports in its Table 8.
  • External reproduction check. Re-trained from scratch on the published recipe and re-scored, our implementation reproduces the published ordering and values within 4–10% — their LoRA 3.24 vs our 3.57 (same recipe), their PiSSA 6.07 vs our 6.31 (ours at α=2r vs their α=r) — and the PiSSA-to-LoRA ratio within 6% (1.77 ours vs 1.87 theirs).
