Magnitude-law campaign · results summary

The Magnitude Law: Update Size Is the First-Order Predictor of Forgetting in PEFT

A controlled study of nine LoRA-family adapters on commonsense reasoning and math. The size of the weight update predicts forgetting; at matched update size the geometric methods land on the same curve as a tuned plain-LoRA baseline, and they cost more to run — so LoRA + weight-decay is the best efficient operating point.

The adapters

All nine methods share the same form — a trainable low-rank update ΔW = A·B added on top of the frozen pretrained weights. What distinguishes them is only how that update is initialized or constrained, not the A·B form itself.

Baselines

LoRA — the standard low-rank adapter; A random, B zero, so the update starts at zero and grows freely. No geometric constraint; its size is governed by learning rate, rank and training length.
LoRA + weight decay ours — plain LoRA trained with AdamW weight decay on the adapter factors A, B. The decay shrinks the L2 (Frobenius) norm of those trainable factors; since ΔW is built from A·B, this indirectly reduces the effective update's functional magnitude (our measured FΔ) — it does not penalize ΔW directly. It imposes no geometric structure (unlike the SVD-init methods below): the same rank-r LoRA parametrization plus one scalar knob that trades a little task fit for less forgetting, at essentially zero extra cost.
DoRA — splits each weight into a magnitude and a direction, applying the LoRA update to the direction and learning the magnitude separately; designed to close the gap to full fine-tuning, not specifically to reduce forgetting.

SVD-initialized

PiSSA — initializes the adapter from the base weight's principal (largest) singular directions; training begins on the most influential directions, so updates tend to be large and, in our study, forget the most.
MiLoRA — initializes from the minor (smallest) singular directions, on the premise that learning in the least-important directions leaves dominant pretrained knowledge undisturbed.

Data-aware / constrained

CLoRA — adds an orthogonality penalty during training that pushes the update into a null space of size k; larger k constrains more directions and shrinks the effective update.
SC-LoRA — initializes inside a subspace computed from calibration data to preserve target knowledge, with a scalar β trading preservation against freedom; the constraint is set once at init and (per its paper) erodes during training.
LoRA-Null — initializes in the null space of calibration activations, so the update's outputs start orthogonal to the directions that carry preserved knowledge.
CorDA++ — a context-oriented SVD weighted by calibration-activation covariance, plus dynamic per-layer rank; can run in a knowledge-preserving mode that adapts the least data-relevant directions.

Magnitude analysis

Plot retention against the learning rate and the adapters look different — they peak at different rates. Re-plot the same runs against the weight-update size they actually produced and they collapse onto one descending curve. Update size, not the choice of adapter, is what predicts forgetting.

One line per adapter across the 7 swept rates (2e-5, 5e-5, 1e-4, 2e-4, 3e-4, 5e-4, 1e-3). They cross and separate — LR is a weak, method-specific proxy (R² = 0.32).
Retention vs update size (FΔ)
Same runs, x-axis swapped. One descending trend across adapters (r = −0.86, R² = 0.74).

The relationship is not an artifact of one setting or a few points. It reproduces within every one of the seven assessed adapters (r −0.86 to −0.97), on a second architecture (Qwen2.5-7B commonsense, LoRA arm, r = −0.88; other adapters and the math axis are not part of the assessed set on this model), and — decisively — the update size is a far better predictor of retention than the learning rate that produced it (R² 0.74 vs 0.32).

The curve is ceiling-shaped, and the robust statistics are stronger than the straight line

Retention cannot exceed the base model's ceiling (26.0), so the left of the curve flattens. Accounting for that only strengthens the result: rank correlation Spearman ρ = −0.90; a saturating fit beats both a line and a parabola on cross-validation, with its knee at update size ≈ 0.37 — essentially LoRA+wd's operating point; and below the ceiling the true slope is ≈ −21 pp per decade of update size (vs −15 for the ceiling-attenuated linear fit). The law survives controlling for adapter identity (partial r = −0.87) and a permutation test (p < 5×10⁻⁵).

The same law appears in CLoRA's own published table

CLoRA's Table 4 reports an update-size metric (FΔ, identical to ours) and out-of-domain accuracy per method. Fitting the law to their ten published rows gives r = −0.98, slope −14.7 pp/decade — against our −14.8. Two independent datasets, two pipelines, the same slope (their FΔ levels run ~2× lower, so it is a parallel law rather than one literal line).

Geometry analysis

Every adapter in §0 differs from plain LoRA in essentially one design choice: where in the weight space it places its update. This section answers the question that choice implies — the question anyone adopting one of these methods is implicitly betting on: does the placement itself protect knowledge, or only the size of the update? We answer it from the trained weights directly, by reconstructing every update (ΔW = (α/r)·B·A, validated against the recorded values) and measuring where its energy went.

Three results. (1) Each method's advertised geometry is real — it survives training and is readable off the saved weights, which both verifies our ports and makes the design claims testable. (2) Once update size is accounted for, placement adds no measurable retention benefit among the methods on the law (rank remains a modest second-order effect). (3) The two methods that fall below the size law do so because of their placement — it concentrates a large, mis-allocated update. So geometry acts through the size and allocation of the update — it sets where on the magnitude axis a method starts — rather than as an independent protection mechanism alongside it.

How we measured it — from the saved weights alone

What we measured — the metrics from first principles. Two different counts appear here, so to be precise: a Llama-2 run adapts 160 weight matrices (5 module types × 32 layers), and we analyze 320 saved adapter checkpoints (runs), each containing its own 160 trained updates. The base weights are frozen and shared by every run, so we take one SVD per matrix — 160 SVDs, computed once — and read all 320 runs against the same directions. The SVD orders each base W's left singular vectors (directions in output space) and right singular vectors (directions in input space) by singular value — by how much of W's action flows through each. "Top-256" = the 256 most important of those directions; "bottom-256" = the 256 least important. Each subspace is 256 of 4096 dimensions, so an update with no directional preference would land ~6% of itself in each — that 0.06 is the neutral reference every number below is read against (exact for the 4096-dim sides; the MLP up/down projections have one 11008-dim side where the neutral value is ≈ 0.023, so 0.06 is the reference for the dominant square matrices). Then, for each of the 320 runs, we reconstruct its 160 updates ΔW = (α/r)·B·A from the stored factors (no retraining) and ask, matrix by matrix, where the energy went.

"Energy" means squared Frobenius norm, ‖ΔW‖²F — the natural additive measure of "how much update" lives where. e_top = ‖projection of ΔW onto the base's top-256 output directions‖²F / ‖ΔW‖²F — the fraction of the update's total energy landing in those directions (e_bot: same for the bottom-256). ein_top / ein_bot repeat this on the input side, using the base's right (input) singular directions. So "how similar are the update's directions to the base's?" is answered by projection: energy in a subspace is exactly the part of ΔW that survives projecting onto it — no angle heuristics. stable rank = ‖ΔW‖²F / σ²max(ΔW): the effective number of directions the update spreads over (1 = one direction dominates; larger = more spread out). law residual = measured retention minus what the magnitude law predicts at that run's FΔ, in pp — positive means the run forgets less than its size alone predicts.

Why these are the right candidates. Each metric directly tests a method's own design claim: MiLoRA claims minor-direction updates (→ e_bot / ein_bot), PiSSA principal directions (→ e_top), SC-LoRA input-principal alignment (→ ein_top), LoRA-Null an input-side null space — and the law residual then asks whether achieving that geometry buys any retention beyond update size. They are computable from the saved adapters alone, with no retraining, and the ΔW reconstruction they rest on is validated to <0.2% against the spectral norms recorded at eval time. The full battery holds a few more fields — amp_top (MiLoRA's own Table-7 principal-block amplification, ‖Utopᵀ·ΔW·Vtop‖F/‖ΔW‖F), an entropy-based effective rank, and the raw Frobenius/spectral norms; the table shows the design-relevant subset, while the "adds nothing beyond size" stress test below controlled for the full battery.

Two levers, ranked

First lever — size. Retention tracks update size within every method (r −0.75 to −0.94 across the battery). Second — rank. At matched update size, higher-rank (more spread-out) updates forget somewhat more (partial r ≈ −0.56, and −0.5 to −0.6 across alternative rank measures); this is largely the adapter rank r itself, so we state it as "size first, rank second," not a hidden axis. Subspace alignment adds essentially nothing measurable beyond these (controlling for size and method, ΔR² ≈ 0.0002): among the on-curve adapters, magnitude rather than subspace alignment sets where a run lands.

Geometry as a fingerprint — recovering each method's design from its trained weights

The alignment metrics cleanly recover what each method was designed to do, and the design persists through three epochs of training. This is the constructive payoff: it identifies each method and explains, mechanistically, why the two methods with the strongest input-side alignment spikes (SC-LoRA, CorDA) are the two that fall below the magnitude law.

Boundary condition — high-rank CLoRA on commonsense

In CLoRA's published results, its k1024/k2048 configurations reach 82.6/83.7 accuracy at BBH 36.5/38.7 — above our LoRA+wd, and their k2048 even exceeds the base model out-of-domain. What keeps this a boundary rather than a refutation: high-k CLoRA sits on the magnitude law — it achieves that retention by shrinking the update (their own Table 4 shows k2048 at the smallest FΔ in their study). So the defensible statement is narrow: at high rank, CLoRA's null-space constraint buys adaptation-efficiency per unit of update that pure size control does not — while the magnitude law still governs both.

Compute & memory per adapter

The fancy methods are not free. Beyond the accuracy–retention picture, they add initialization work, training-time memory, or wall-clock — for the same trainable-parameter budget. LoRA+wd sits on the efficiency frontier at zero added cost.

Reading the costs concretely. The SVD-init methods (MiLoRA, PiSSA) factor all 160 base matrices in a few minutes — negligible. The calibration methods run real 7B forward passes before training: LoRA-Null needs ~256 and SC-LoRA ~512 (256 preserve + 256 target), a few minutes each on a B200. During those passes they hold a ~22 GB activation-covariance buffer — in addition to the model weights already loaded for the calibration — but it exists only at initialization: it is freed before training starts, never becomes part of the adapter, and costs nothing at train or inference time. CorDA++ needs 1,280 passes (5 sampling rounds) and, per its own paper (Table IX), spends ~1 GPU-h precomputing — roughly 20% on top of the ~5 GPU-h training run. DoRA instead pays 2.1× the wall-clock at every step. CLoRA's cost is memory, and it is additional to the shared ~55 GB footprint: a frozen regularization block of +0.42 GB at k=128 rising to +6.7 GB at k=2048 (at the default k=512, +1.7 GB — already 8× the trainable LoRA weights) for no added capacity. LoRA and LoRA+wd pay none of these — zero init, no extra memory, plain-LoRA wall-clock.

Forgetting, measured the field's own way

As an independent check we implemented MiLoRA's own forgetting metric — the cross-entropy of the fine-tuned model against the base model on WikiText-103 (following Kalajdzievski, 2024). It reproduces their published numbers and independently confirms the magnitude law on a metric we did not design.

How the metric is computed. WikiText-103's test set — neutral text none of these models was ever fine-tuned on — is concatenated and chunked into non-overlapping 1024-token blocks; we score 40 blocks × 1,023 next-token positions each = 40,920 scored positions per adapter. At every position, both models predict a full next-token probability distribution: the frozen base model (obtained by disabling the adapter on the same wrapped model, so it is exactly the pretrained weights the update was added to) and the fine-tuned model. The metric is the soft cross-entropy between the two distributions, CEt = −Σv pbase(v | context) · log pft(v | context), averaged over all positions. That is ordinary next-token training loss with the hard target label replaced by the base model's distribution — so it reads directly as "how far has the model's next-token behavior drifted from base on neutral text," not as hard-label perplexity. The definition follows Kalajdzievski (2024), and it is precisely the metric MiLoRA's paper reports in its Table 8; our implementation reproduces their published values on the same recipe — their LoRA 3.24 vs our 3.57, their PiSSA 6.07 vs our 6.31.
