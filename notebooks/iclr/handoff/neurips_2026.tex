\begin{abstract}
Many fine-tuning methods use the singular value decomposition of pretrained weights to choose which directions to adapt. Can a model learn when its updates stay entirely in the lower spectral tail, the directions with smaller singular values? Does allowing those directions to interact improve adaptation? We introduce a new family of adapters that separates three choices: changing individual tail coefficients, combining directions within the tail, and allowing interaction with leading directions. Our analysis explains the freedom each choice provides and how to control it. In RoBERTa experiments on RTE, training only tail coefficients raises accuracy from 60\% with a trained classifier alone to 70\%. Leading and middle directions reach similar accuracy, and rotations give no consistent further gain. In a separate experiment allowing both leading and tail updates, restricting their interaction roughly halves held-out prediction loss on RTE and MRPC compared with a size penalty tuned for similar update sizes, while mean accuracy changes by less than half a percentage point. In these experiments, accuracy is similar across subspace choices and interaction settings, while interaction control can improve the probabilities assigned to answers.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Fine-tuning adapts a pretrained model to a new task. A growing family of methods uses the singular value decomposition (SVD) of pretrained weights to choose which directions to train. Some begin with the leading directions, associated with larger singular values. Others begin with the lower spectral tail, associated with smaller values, or learn coefficients and rotations in a fixed singular-vector basis~\citep{meng2024pissa,wang2025milora,han2023svdiff,lingam2024svft,zhang2024spectral,wu2026psoft}. These approaches make the pretrained spectrum part of the adaptation design.

Choosing directions is one part of that design. Another is deciding how they may interact during training. Can the lower tail alone support adaptation? Does combining its directions improve learning? What changes when leading and tail directions can interact? We study these questions by separating where an update is allowed from how freely it can change that region, and measuring the resulting prediction loss and accuracy.

We introduce a family of adapters that separates these choices. LinLoRA learns one coefficient per selected tail direction, changing its singular value. OrthoLoRA adds rotations that shuffle different directions within the tail. Both leave the remaining pretrained directions unchanged. Our analysis quantifies which additional weight changes rotations can express.

Their scaled versions, UILinLoRA and UIOrthoLoRA, also learn diagonal matrices for each input and output coordinate of an adapted layer. These diagonal scalers can shuffle tail and leading directions. We derive an interaction penalty that discourages this shuffling while allowing changes within either group.

We run two studies with RoBERTa-base. The first tests tail adaptation on RTE (sentence entailment). We attach a small classifier after RoBERTa's final layer to map its sentence-pair representation to label probabilities. We train this classifier either alone or together with LinLoRA or OrthoLoRA adapters in all 48 attention projections. Adapters use 256 tail directions; the pretrained weights remain frozen. \\
The tail supports useful adaptation beyond the classifier alone while leading directions stay fixed. Leading and middle directions also support adaptation, showing that useful learning is possible in each tested spectral region. Rotations add flexibility but give no consistent further accuracy gain (Table~\ref{tab:tailcapacity}). Section~\ref{sec:tailstudy} describes the training and evaluation design.

The second study asks whether controlling interaction offers more than shrinking weight changes. On RTE and MRPC (paraphrase detection), we train UILinLoRA with both leading and tail updates and the same classifier design. We compare no penalty, our interaction penalty, and a size penalty tuned to give similar update sizes. Interaction control gives lower held-out prediction loss than either alternative, while mean accuracy changes little (Table~\ref{tab:learningandmixing} and Figure~\ref{fig:taskoutcomes}). The gain lies in the probabilities assigned to answers and would be missed by looking only at accuracy. Section~\ref{sec:matchedcontrol} gives the training and evaluation design.

The penalties also produce different update patterns. Interaction control reduces the share of weight change connecting leading and tail directions; shrinking updates leaves that share high (Figure~\ref{fig:crossvsnorm}). Update size alone therefore misses how adaptation is distributed across directions. This loss improvement is achieved while both leading and tail directions are allowed to change.

\section{A family of controlled spectral adapters}
\label{sec:confinement}

\subsection{Start with a fixed spectral tail}
\label{sec:coordinates}
For a square pretrained weight matrix $W_0\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$, write
\begin{equation}
W_0=U\Sigma V^\top=U_L\Sigma_LV_L^\top+U_T\Sigma_TV_T^\top.
\label{eq:pretrained}
\end{equation}
Singular values are ordered from largest to smallest. We call the first $k$ directions leading ($L$), and the remaining $r=d-k$ directions the tail ($T$). The cutoff divides the pretrained directions into two groups; both can have nonzero singular values. We keep these original bases fixed throughout training and analysis of the confined adapters. Appendix~\ref{app:rectangular} extends the construction to rectangular weights.

An adapted layer is $W_{\mathrm{eff}}=W_0+\Delta$. To guarantee that its update stays in the selected tail, use
\begin{equation}
\Delta=U_T H V_T^\top.
\label{eq:tailcore}
\end{equation}
Only the small matrix $H$ is learned. Multiplication by the fixed tail bases keeps every update inside the selected region. We can therefore change the freedom of $H$ while holding the allowed region constant.

\subsection{Change coefficients, then allow rotations}
The first adapter sets $H=\diag(h)$, with $h\in\mathbb R^r$ trainable. It changes the strength of each selected pair of pretrained directions independently. We use LinLoRA as its implementation label. SVDiff and SVFT already establish fixed-basis coefficient tuning~\citep{han2023svdiff,lingam2024svft}; SVFT also learns selected off-diagonal coefficients. Here the diagonal family provides a simple restricted reference.

The second adapter allows two learned orthogonal rotations inside the last $q\le r$ tail directions. Split the tail into $r-q$ fixed directions ($M$) and $q$ rotatable directions ($S$), and set
\begin{equation}
H=\begin{bmatrix}
\diag(h_M)&0\\0&R_U\diag(h_S)R_V^\top
\end{bmatrix},\qquad R_U,R_V\in\mathbb O(q).
\label{eq:rotations}
\end{equation}
This is the OrthoLoRA construction. At $q=0$, it reduces to fixed coefficients. At $q=r$, arbitrary coefficients and the inner SVD can represent any real tail matrix $H$. Intermediate values give more freedom to a smaller block. The implementation settings $k_{\mathrm{val}}=r$ and $k_{\mathrm{vec}}=q$ specify these two dimensions. The rotation size $q$ controls the added freedom and the number of trainable parameters. Our controlled study enforces orthogonality through a matrix exponential (Appendix~\ref{app:bandstudy}).

Both adapters remain inside the same tail. The difference is whether a tail input direction can connect only to its paired output direction or also to other output directions in the tail. This isolates flexibility within the selected region.

\subsection{Separate location from flexibility}
The following result quantifies the extra freedom supplied by a full tail core. Let $G$ be a target weight update; $\|\cdot\|_F$ denotes the Frobenius norm, the square root of the sum of squared entries.

\begin{proposition}[Two distinct approximation bottlenecks]
\label{prop:approximation}
For an arbitrary target matrix $G\in\mathbb R^{d\times d}$, let $G_{TT}=U_T^\top G V_T$. Then
\begin{align}
\min_h\fro{G-U_T\diag(h)V_T^\top}^2
&=\fro{G}^2-\sum_{i=1}^r |(G_{TT})_{ii}|^2,\label{eq:fixederror}\\
\min_H\fro{G-U_T H V_T^\top}^2
&=\fro{G}^2-\fro{G_{TT}}^2.\label{eq:fullerror}
\end{align}
The improvement in the best attainable squared error equals the off-diagonal energy of $G_{TT}$. Both families retain the same unavoidable outside-tail residual.
\end{proposition}


For example, connecting one tail input direction to a different tail output direction requires an off-diagonal entry of $H$. A full tail core can express that connection. Diagonal coefficients leave it out. Proposition~\ref{prop:approximation} separates this within-tail restriction from the residual outside the selected region. The proof follows by orthogonal projection (Appendix~\ref{app:approximation}). Training determines which of the permitted updates is learned.

Confinement is defined relative to the original layer coordinates. For the same input $x$, $U_L^\top(W_0+\Delta)x=U_L^\top W_0x$. The network still learns through the changed tail outputs and through inputs altered by preceding layers. The original leading directions remain top-ranked when
\begin{equation}
\sigma_k(W_0)>\|\Sigma_T+H\|_2.
\label{eq:nocrossing}
\end{equation}
Appendix~\ref{app:crossing} gives a counterexample when this separation fails.

\section{Controlling leading--tail interaction}
\label{sec:mixing}

The confined families test what can be learned inside the tail. To study a relaxation, add diagonal output and input scalers $E,D$. They act in the layer's original coordinates, rather than in its singular-vector basis. Consequently, $EU_THV_T^\top D$ can affect directions outside the tail.

The scaled adapter used in our main experiment also includes a leading core. We write both choices in one form:
\begin{equation}
\Delta=E\bigl(U_LS_LV_L^\top+U_THV_T^\top\bigr)D,
\quad S_L=\begin{cases}0&\text{tail core only},\\I&\text{practical adapter}.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:unified}
\end{equation}
The expression is added to $W_0$. Thus $S_L=I$ adds an identity core in the leading coordinates, and learning $E,D$ changes its contribution along with the tail contribution. We call this the practical adapter. UILinLoRA uses diagonal $H$; UIOrthoLoRA also includes the rotations. Our completed interaction experiment uses the practical UILinLoRA form.

\subsection{Identify the interactions to control}
We inspect the effective update in the original pretrained basis:
\begin{equation}
C=U^\top\Delta V=
\begin{bmatrix}C_{LL}&C_{LT}\\C_{TL}&C_{TT}\end{bmatrix}.
\label{eq:coordinates}
\end{equation}
$C_{TT}$ records tail-to-tail changes, including any within-tail combinations. $C_{LL}$ records changes among leading directions. The two cross blocks link the leading and tail groups. Strict tail confinement leaves only $C_{TT}$. Decoupling the two groups instead leaves both within-group blocks, $C_{LL}$ and $C_{TT}$.

Let $A=U^\top EU$, $B=V^\top DV$, and define the two scaler-mixing operators
\begin{equation}
M_E=U_L^\top EU_T,\qquad M_D=V_L^\top DV_T,\qquad
\mu=\|M_E\|_2,\quad\nu=\|M_D\|_2.
\label{eq:mixingoperators}
\end{equation}
The norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ is the operator norm. Direct multiplication gives
\begin{equation}
C_{ab}=A_{aL}S_LB_{Lb}+A_{aT}HB_{Tb},\qquad a,b\in\{L,T\}.
\label{eq:blockidentity}
\end{equation}
The two operators measure how the scalers connect the groups. When both are zero, the cross blocks vanish. A tail-only core then recovers strict confinement. The practical core retains its leading block: for example, $E=D=I,H=0$ gives $C_{LL}=I$. With bounded factor norms, small mixing operators bound the cross blocks as stated in Proposition~\ref{prop:mixing} and Corollary~\ref{cor:confinement} (Appendix~\ref{app:mixingproof}).

\subsection{How the interaction penalty works}
We train with
\begin{equation}
\mathcal L=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{task}}+
\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal M}\left(\lambda_E\|M_{E,\ell}\|_F^2+
\lambda_D\|M_{D,\ell}\|_F^2\right),
\label{eq:regularizer}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal M$ contains the adapted matrices. The two coefficients control the pressure on output-side and input-side interaction. Task training and the interaction penalty jointly determine the update.

The penalty's mechanism is explicit. Write $P=U_LU_L^\top$ and $E=\diag(\epsilon)$. Then
\begin{equation}
\|M_E\|_F^2=\tfrac12\|EP-PE\|_F^2
=\sum_{i<j}P_{ij}^2(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j)^2.
\label{eq:commutator}
\end{equation}
It penalizes differences between scaling factors, with weights determined by the pretrained projector. If the associated graph is connected, its zero-penalty solutions are uniform scalers. Isolated penalty dynamics then approach $E=aI,D=bI$. For the practical core this gives $C_{LL}=abI$, $C_{TT}=abH$, and zero cross blocks. This gives the mechanism of the penalty: it suppresses connections between the groups while retaining a uniformly scaled leading contribution. During adaptation, task gradients act alongside this pressure. Our experiment measures the resulting interaction, loss and accuracy.

We compare this intervention with penalizing the size of the effective update. The two objectives act on different quantities: one penalizes scaler interactions, while the other penalizes total displacement from the pretrained weights. Their behavior during factor training is analyzed in Appendices~\ref{app:commutator}--\ref{app:factorflow}. We evaluate the final effective update directly, which also accounts for reciprocal rescalings of the adapter factors.

\section{Measuring adaptation}
\label{sec:measurement}

We evaluate behavior and weight changes at the same saved checkpoint. Behavior tells us how well the adapted model predicts. The weight measurements show what the adapter changed to produce those predictions.

\paragraph{Prediction loss and accuracy.}
Accuracy counts correct predicted labels. Prediction loss is cross-entropy, the mean negative log probability assigned to the correct label. Two models can predict the same label while assigning it different probabilities, so loss can reveal a difference that accuracy misses. We report both on held-out task examples at the fixed training endpoint. The three paired training seeds are the units of replication; tables give means and sample standard deviations, with paired differences in the appendix.

\paragraph{Interaction between leading and tail directions.}
The four blocks in~\eqref{eq:coordinates} partition the squared update norm:
\begin{equation}
\|\Delta\|_F^2=\sum_{a,b\in\{L,T\}}\|C_{ab}\|_F^2.
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
For a nonzero update, $p_{ab}=\|C_{ab}\|_F^2/\|\Delta\|_F^2$ is the fraction of energy in a block. We call $p_\cross=p_{LT}+p_{TL}$ the cross share: it measures the share connecting leading and tail directions. The total share outside the tail is $p_\off=p_{LL}+p_\cross$. Off-diagonal entries \emph{inside} $C_{TT}$ instead describe combinations within the tail. These measurements distinguish the three choices in our adapter family. Fractions at an exactly zero update are undefined.

\paragraph{Overall size and distribution across the network.}
Relative update size is $\rho_F=\|\Delta\|_F/\|W_0\|_F$. We use it to compare interaction control with reducing the overall amount of weight change. For the network-wide summary, we sum squared energies across adapted matrices before taking ratios. We also report each matrix's norm, the equal-module mean of block fractions, and the cumulative energy carried by the largest updates. These views distinguish a broadly distributed change from one concentrated in a few matrices. Appendix~\ref{app:details} gives the exact aggregations, including the archived ratio $q_\off=\sqrt{p_\off}$.

\paragraph{Changes learned after insertion.}
The practical adapter starts with a nonzero contribution. We therefore measure both its total displacement from the pretrained weights, $\Delta=W_{\mathrm{eff}}-W_0$, and its displacement during training, $\Delta_{\mathrm{learned}}=W_{\mathrm{eff}}-W_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)$. We subtract matrices before computing energies. Within the learned leading block, we also separate its scalar identity component from the remaining structure. This connects the measured endpoint to the uniform-scaler limit derived in Section~\ref{sec:mixing}.

\paragraph{A separate masked-token behavior.}
We evaluate a fixed masked-token probe using the original frozen language-model head. It measures a pretraining-style behavior on the same adapted checkpoints. The task head and language-model head test different predictions; their losses are reported separately.

\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{The tail supports adaptation beyond the trained head}
\label{sec:tailstudy}

\subsection{Experimental design for interaction control}
\label{sec:matchedcontrol}
The main experiment uses RoBERTa-base~\citep{liu2019roberta} on RTE, a textual-entailment task, and MRPC, a paraphrase task. We freeze the backbone and train adapters in all 48 attention projections together with the task head. Each $768\times768$ projection has 256 tail coefficients and input/output scalers, with the practical leading core $S_L=I$ and no rotations. Thus each condition can change both leading and tail directions; the intervention controls how those groups interact.

We compare three conditions. UNREG trains only on the task loss. MIX adds the two-sided interaction penalty with $\lambda_E=\lambda_D=10^{-3}$. NORM adds the size penalty $\beta\,\mathrm{mean}_\ell\|\Delta_\ell\|_F^2/\|W_{0,\ell}\|_F^2$. The size control asks whether the effect of MIX is reproduced by making the overall update smaller. Its dose is selected using update size on a separate calibration seed, without using task scores.

Each condition uses confirmation seeds 17, 42 and 123, giving 18 runs across the two tasks. Conditions share initialization, data order, optimizer settings and step budgets within a task and seed. Training ends at 5{,}670 steps on RTE and 2{,}760 on MRPC. After freezing these endpoints, we evaluate once on the previously held-out official validation sets: 277 examples for RTE and 408 for MRPC. All runs pass checkpoint reload and metric-reproduction checks. Appendix~\ref{app:matchedprotocol} records the complete calibration frontier, per-seed matches and inner-selection outcomes; Appendix~\ref{app:freshrecipe} gives the executed recipes.

\subsection{Smaller updates retain substantial interaction}
The two penalties produce different update patterns. At its selected doses, NORM retains $46.2\%$ cross share on both tasks. MIX has $10.0\pm0.5\%$ on RTE and $4.5\pm0.3\%$ on MRPC, compared with the unregularized $43.7\%$ and $42.4\%$. Interaction control therefore changes the relative allocation of the update as well as its size.

The calibration procedure selects $\beta=7.5$ for RTE and $27$ for MRPC. Their overall sizes differ from the MIX targets by $4.1\%$ and $2.3\%$ on the calibration seed. Across confirmation seeds, absolute differences range from $0.1$ to $8.0\%$; Table~\ref{tab:matchedconfirmation} gives each signed error. We retain the frozen doses and all confirmation pairs.

The full seven-dose sweep per task provides a broader view (Figure~\ref{fig:crossvsnorm}). On the calibration seed, NORM reduces update size by $12.1\times$ on RTE and $8.5\times$ on MRPC. Cross share remains $43.7$--$50.9\%$ and $42.4$--$48.6\%$, respectively. None of these tested doses reproduces MIX's low cross share. The distinction is visible across a wide range of achieved sizes, as well as at the selected doses.

\subsection{Interaction control redistributes adaptation}
The global norm summarizes how much changed, but not where the change occurred. On RTE, the three largest MIX updates carry $61.9$--$68.3\%$ of total energy, versus $15.2$--$16.7\%$ for NORM. Between 12 and 15 of MIX's 48 module-relative norms are below $10^{-6}$. On MRPC, its top three modules carry $35.7$--$50.1\%$, versus $17.5$--$19.5\%$ for NORM. Interaction control produces a more concentrated distribution in both tasks. Appendix~\ref{app:focusedmodules} shows the individual matrices and complete cumulative curves.

Leading changes remain after subtracting the insertion state: they account for $45.2\%$ and $65.5\%$ of MIX's pooled learned energy on RTE and MRPC. Of that leading energy, $84.7\%$ and $89.5\%$ lies in module-wise scalar identity components. This is the structure predicted by the uniform-scaler limit. The remaining non-identity leading energy is about $6.9\%$ of total learned energy on both tasks (Appendix~\ref{app:reviewidentity}). Thus interaction suppression coexists with substantial, mostly identity-shaped leading changes.

Equal-module averages and the small-update sensitivity analysis also retain the cross-share gap (Appendix~\ref{app:reviewsensitivity}). Similar global sizes can conceal different module distributions and block allocations.

\subsection{Task adaptation and masked-token behavior differ}
The fixed language-model probe (Appendix~\ref{app:freshrecipe}) has original-backbone loss $2.0956$ nats. Every arm's mean probe loss is $0.47$--$0.84$ nats higher, and MIX has the highest mean on both tasks. Its lower task loss therefore accompanies worse masked-token loss on this probe. Measuring both makes the functional outcome concrete: the favorable result is the held-out task prediction loss.

\section{Relation to spectral adaptation methods}
\label{sec:related}
\paragraph{Choosing directions.}
LoRA restricts update rank~\citep{hu2022lora}; AdaLoRA allocates an update's singular-value budget~\citep{zhang2023adalora}. PiSSA and MiLoRA initialize trainable factors from leading and minor pretrained components~\citep{meng2024pissa,wang2025milora}. KaSA and CorDA make other decomposition-guided choices~\citep{wang2025kasa,yang2024corda}. We use a fixed pretrained reference to distinguish the initial choice from the region occupied by the trained update. Free factors can move beyond their initial spans, while our fixed tail bases enforce confinement throughout training.

\paragraph{Combining directions.}
SVDiff trains spectral shifts, SVFT learns selected combinations of fixed singular vectors, and LoRA-XS trains a small core on fixed support~\citep{han2023svdiff,lingam2024svft,balazy2024lora}. Spectral Adapter rotates a selected leading core, and PSOFT relaxes orthogonality inside principal support~\citep{zhang2024spectral,wu2026psoft}. Our adapter family brings these choices into one controlled construction. The approximation identity separates flexibility within the chosen region from access to directions outside it; the interaction analysis explains how ambient scaling connects the groups. Appendix~\ref{app:methodcoordinates} derives the coordinate forms of these methods.

\paragraph{Rotations, scaling and restricted adaptation.}
OFT/BOFT and HRA use orthogonal transformations~\citep{qiu2023oft,liu2024boft,yuan2024hra}. VeRA, RandLoRA, DoRA, SSF and IA3 use other scaling or factor restrictions~\citep{kopiczko2023vera,albert2025randlora,liu2024dora,lian2022ssf,liu2022ia3}, while FourierFT selects Fourier coefficients~\citep{gao2024parameter}. Work on restricted parameter spaces motivates studying which freedoms matter for adaptation~\citep{li2018measuring,aghajanyan2020intrinsic}. We connect that question to measured weight changes and task behavior, treating behavioral retention~\citep{shuttleworth2025illusion} and ranked-subspace perturbation~\citep{wedin1972perturbation} as separate measurements. Our controlled result compares interaction and size penalties within the same spectral adapter.

\section{Scope and practical considerations}
\label{sec:scope}
The controlled evidence covers one encoder, two tasks, three confirmation seeds and one fixed interaction strength. The loss ordering was identified after inspecting the held-out evaluation. Cross-entropy depends on confidence as well as predicted labels; temperature scaling fitted on separate inner-selection logits is the next check (Appendix~\ref{app:heldloss}). The MIX--NORM accuracy differences have nominal 95\% paired intervals $[-3.92,4.16]$ points on RTE and $[-3.89,3.24]$ on MRPC. The small observed mean differences therefore describe these runs rather than establish equivalent accuracy.

The comparison measures the full effect of the two training penalties. Confirmation size errors reach $8.0\%$, with five of six pairs outside the calibration rule's $5\%$ tolerance, and module allocations differ. The small-update sensitivity analysis conditions on attained norms, a post-treatment variable. Isolating cross share as the cause of the loss difference, or the pretrained projector as the essential ingredient, requires additional controls. The separate 21-run band study uses RTE inner-selection scores; its frozen checkpoints have not yet been evaluated on the official held-out split. It establishes neither tail superiority across tasks nor a convergence advantage. Rotations add parameters as well as flexibility.

The practical base adapter has 86{,}016 trainable adapter entries and stores 108 MiB of frozen bases at two bytes per entry. Its two dense basis products require about 192 times the branch multiply--accumulates of rank-4 LoRA at width 768; this is an arithmetic comparison. Merging the update removes the adapter branch for inference. Appendix~\ref{app:cost} separates setup, storage, training and merge costs. The historical benchmark protocols and configuration-derived parameter counts remain separately labeled.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
Our adapters separate where a model can adapt from how directions can interact. Strict tail coefficients improve RTE accuracy over a trained head alone; adding rotations within that tail does not give a consistent gain. When both leading and tail updates are allowed, interaction control lowers held-out prediction loss by $44\%$ and $48\%$ relative to the tested size penalty, with mean accuracy differences below half a point. Controlling interaction changes how the model adapts, and accuracy alone misses the resulting difference in prediction loss.

\section{Proofs and boundary cases}
\label{app:proofs}

\subsection{Fixed coefficients and within-tail flexibility}
\label{app:approximation}
Let $\widehat G=U^\top G V$ and partition it into the four coordinate blocks. Orthogonal invariance gives
\begin{equation}
\fro{G-U_T H V_T^\top}^2
=\fro{\widehat G_{LL}}^2+\fro{\widehat G_{LT}}^2
+\fro{\widehat G_{TL}}^2+\fro{\widehat G_{TT}-H}^2.
\end{equation}
For a free core the last term is minimized at $H=\widehat G_{TT}$. For a diagonal core it is minimized by $h_i=(\widehat G_{TT})_{ii}$, leaving the off-diagonal entries unchanged. Substitution proves Proposition~\ref{prop:approximation}. An SVD of the minimizing free core gives the representation~\eqref{eq:rotations} with $q=r$. Thus full within-tail rotations can attain the free-core approximation optimum.

For a separate family whose free core has rank at most $q\le r$, rather than the partial-rotation core of~\eqref{eq:rotations}, truncated SVD of $\widehat G_{TT}$ gives minimum squared error
\begin{equation}
\fro{G}^2-\sum_{j=1}^q\sigma_j(\widehat G_{TT})^2.
\end{equation}
This expression keeps the outside-tail residual explicit. It is the optimum under the fixed-tail restriction; the unrestricted rank-$q$ approximation of $G$ can use directions elsewhere.

\subsection{Proof of the unified mixing bounds}
\label{app:mixingproof}
\begin{proposition}[Interaction bounds with and without a leading core]
\label{prop:mixing}
Write $e=\op{E}$, $d_s=\op{D}$, $s=\op{S_L}$, and $h_s=\op{H}$. For~\eqref{eq:unified},
\begin{align}
\op{C_{LL}}&\le e s d_s+\mu h_s\nu, &
\op{C_{LT}}&\le e s\nu+\mu h_s d_s,\label{eq:blockbound1}\\
\op{C_{TL}}&\le\mu s d_s+e h_s\nu, &
\op{C_{TT}}&\le\mu s\nu+e h_s d_s.\label{eq:blockbound2}
\end{align}
If $\mu=\nu=0$, the cross blocks vanish. In addition, the leading block vanishes when $S_L=0$.
\end{proposition}

The proof applies submultiplicativity to~\eqref{eq:blockidentity}. The additional leading-core terms are the difference between the idealized and practical analyses. With $S_L=0$, leading--leading leakage requires mixing on both sides, while each cross block can be first order in one mixing quantity. With $S_L\ne0$, direct leading adaptation remains even when the cross terms vanish.

\begin{corollary}[Tail confinement and block decoupling]
\label{cor:confinement}
For $S_L=0$, define $C_{\mathrm{tail}}=\diag(0,C_{TT})$. Then
\begin{equation}
\op{C-C_{\mathrm{tail}}}
\le h_s\sqrt{(\mu\nu)^2+(\mu d_s)^2+(e\nu)^2}.
\label{eq:offbound}
\end{equation}
Consequently, $\mu,\nu\to0$ implies confinement in absolute block norm if $e,d_s,h_s$ stay bounded. If $e,d_s,h_s,s$ stay bounded for a general leading core, the same limit instead makes $C$ block-diagonal; it need not make $C_{LL}$ small.
\end{corollary}


Because $U,V$ are orthogonal,
\begin{equation}
C=A\begin{bmatrix}S_L&0\\0&H\end{bmatrix}B.
\end{equation}
Direct multiplication gives
\begin{align}
C_{LL}&=A_{LL}S_LB_{LL}+A_{LT}HB_{TL},\\
C_{LT}&=A_{LL}S_LB_{LT}+A_{LT}HB_{TT},\\
C_{TL}&=A_{TL}S_LB_{LL}+A_{TT}HB_{TL},\\
C_{TT}&=A_{TL}S_LB_{LT}+A_{TT}HB_{TT}.
\end{align}
Symmetry yields $\op{A_{TL}}=\op{A_{LT}}=\mu$ and $\op{B_{TL}}=\op{B_{LT}}=\nu$. Orthogonal compression bounds the diagonal blocks of $A$ by $e$ and those of $B$ by $d_s$. Apply the triangle inequality and submultiplicativity to each product to obtain~\eqref{eq:blockbound1}--\eqref{eq:blockbound2}.

For the tail-only core, $s=0$ and
\begin{equation}
C-C_{\mathrm{tail}}=\begin{bmatrix}C_{LL}&C_{LT}\\C_{TL}&0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
For any block vector $(x,y)$, the norms of the two output blocks are bounded componentwise by
\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix}\op{C_{LL}}&\op{C_{LT}}\\\op{C_{TL}}&0\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}\|x\|_2\\\|y\|_2\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Taking a supremum and bounding the norm of this nonnegative $2\times2$ matrix by its Frobenius norm proves
\begin{equation}
\op{C-C_{\mathrm{tail}}}
\le\sqrt{\op{C_{LL}}^2+\op{C_{LT}}^2+\op{C_{TL}}^2}.
\end{equation}
Substitution gives~\eqref{eq:offbound}. If the stated factor norms remain bounded, its right-hand side goes to zero as $\mu,\nu\to0$.

For the general core, let $C_{\mathrm{diag}}=\diag(C_{LL},C_{TT})$. The off-diagonal block matrix satisfies the exact identity
\begin{equation}
\op{C-C_{\mathrm{diag}}}=\max\{\op{C_{LT}},\op{C_{TL}}\}.
\end{equation}
The cross-block bounds therefore imply convergence to the set of block-diagonal updates when $e,d_s,h_s,s$ are bounded and $\mu,\nu\to0$. The term $A_{LL}S_LB_{LL}$ need not vanish. This proves Corollary~\ref{cor:confinement} and states precisely the different limits in the two cases.

\subsection{Confinement does not prevent singular-value crossings}
\label{app:crossing}
For a tail-only update without ambient scaling,
\begin{equation}
U^\top(W_0+\Delta)V=
\begin{bmatrix}\Sigma_L&0\\0&\Sigma_T+H\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
The singular values are the union of those of the two diagonal blocks, which proves the sufficient condition~\eqref{eq:nocrossing}. Consider $W_0=\diag(2,1)$, cutoff $k=1$, and $\Delta=\diag(0,2)$. The update is strictly tail-supported with no cross mixing and no leading-block modification, but $W_0+\Delta=\diag(2,3)$ has the other coordinate as its top singular direction. Its largest-principal-angle sine relative to the original leading direction is one. Thus block decoupling and stability of the top-ranked subspace are distinct even in a two-dimensional example.

\subsection{Rectangular weights and complete energy accounting}
\label{app:rectangular}
For $W_0\in\mathbb R^{m\times n}$, select $k\le\min(m,n)$ leading singular vectors and define
\begin{equation}
P_L=U_LU_L^\top,\quad P_T=I_m-P_L,\qquad
Q_L=V_LV_L^\top,\quad Q_T=I_n-Q_L.
\end{equation}
Then $\Delta_{ab}=P_a\Delta Q_b$ gives
\begin{equation}
\Delta=\sum_{a,b}\Delta_{ab},\qquad
\fro{\Delta}^2=\sum_{a,b}\fro{\Delta_{ab}}^2.
\end{equation}
Pairwise Frobenius inner products vanish because complementary projectors multiply to zero. The tail here is the full complement and includes any unmatched null directions. A chosen adapter may span only a subset of it; that available subspace must not be confused with the diagnostic complement. Complete orthogonal bases recover the corresponding coordinate blocks. The square attention-matrix experiment avoids this issue, but an economy-SVD diagnostic is not automatically exhaustive for other layers.

\subsection{Representative methods in the fixed coordinate frame}

These are algebraic consequences of update forms, not measurements on prior methods. Let $Q_J$ select the declared pretrained singular indices $J$, and write $A_a=U_a^\top A$, $B_b=B V_b$. Any additive update $AB$ has $C_{ab}=A_aB_b$; low rank alone forces no LL/LT/TL/TT block to vanish. A learned sum of low-rank terms has the sum of these blocks. A fixed-basis core instead has $\Delta=UQ_JKQ_J^\top V^\top$ and $C=Q_JKQ_J^\top$, so only its selected support is reachable.

For LoRA and AdaLoRA the low-rank factors are trainable~\citep{hu2022lora,zhang2023adalora}. PiSSA/MiLoRA's retained residual means displacement from the original checkpoint subtracts the initial product; the leading/minor initialization does not freeze the factor spans~\citep{meng2024pissa,wang2025milora}. In LoRA-XS the published initialization includes $\Sigma_J$ in the left fixed factor, hence $C_{JJ}=\Sigma_JR$, not literally $R$ unless it is reparameterized~\citep{balazy2024lora}. If $J$ lies inside the declared leading set, every other diagnostic block is zero.

The Spectral Adapter entry describes its rotational leading-core variant~\citep{zhang2024spectral}. OFT/BOFT and HRA use orthogonal $R$ in the pure forms shown~\citep{qiu2023oft,liu2024boft,yuan2024hra}; singular-value preservation is compatible with nonzero cross blocks. For PSOFT, $A'=U_J$, $B'=\Sigma_JV_J^\top$, and $K_P=\diag(\alpha)R\diag(\beta)$ act inside a fixed principal core; the residual of $W_0$ stays frozen~\citep{wu2026psoft}. Thus $C_{JJ}=(K_P-I)\Sigma_J$, with zero support outside $J\times J$ even after vector relaxation. Its geometry condition $R^\top A^\top AR=A^\top A$ preserves column Gram geometry; choosing $A'=U_J$ reduces it to orthogonality. Our Corollary~\ref{cor:confinement} instead bounds support outside specified blocks. Neither property alone implies the other: a nonorthogonal core remains confined, while an ambient orthogonal map can mix blocks. Principal versus tail selection is a further independent choice.

GaLore projects gradients into low-rank subspaces that can change during training~\citep{zhao2024galore}; the sum of such updates need not remain inside one fixed pretrained span. GOFT/qGOFT use Givens rotations and relaxed angular/norm constraints~\citep{ma2024qgoft}, complementing the ambient-transform examples above. These differ from a fixed update-support constraint.

\subsection{A dimension-only reference for allocation}
\label{app:dimension}
Let the vectorized nonzero update have a rotationally invariant direction in the $d^2$-dimensional matrix space. By symmetry each squared coordinate has expected fraction $1/d^2$ of the total. The leading, cross, and tail blocks have $k^2$, $2kr$, and $r^2$ coordinates, respectively. Their expected energy fractions are therefore $k^2/d^2$, $2kr/d^2$, and $r^2/d^2$. For $r/d=1/3$, these are $4/9$, $4/9$, and $1/9$, with off-tail expectation $8/9$. The square root $\sqrt{8/9}\simeq0.9428$ is a reference on the norm-ratio scale, not the expectation $\mathbb E[q_{\off}]$. These calculations are a null reference, not a fitted model or a statistical test of the observed updates.

\subsection{The scaler penalty as a projector commutator}
\label{app:commutator}
In the complete $U$ basis, $P$ is $\diag(I_k,0)$. Writing $A=U^\top EU$ gives
\begin{equation}
U^\top(EP-PE)U=
\begin{bmatrix}0&-A_{LT}\\A_{TL}&0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}
Because $A$ is symmetric, orthogonal invariance yields $\|EP-PE\|_F^2=2\|A_{LT}\|_F^2$. In ambient coordinates, the $(i,j)$ entry of the commutator is $(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j)P_{ij}$. Squaring and combining its symmetric pairs proves~\eqref{eq:commutator}. Equivalently, the penalty is $\epsilon^\top L_P\epsilon$, where $L_P$ is the graph Laplacian with off-diagonal edge weights $P_{ij}^2$. Every summand is nonnegative. Zero penalty therefore means equal scales across every nonzero edge, or constant scales on each connected component. A connected graph has only globally constant scales in this nullspace. A disconnected projector permits different constants on disconnected components; for a coordinate-aligned projector, all off-diagonal weights vanish and every ambient diagonal scaler commutes with it. The result is conditional on the fixed pretrained projector, not a measured connectivity claim for the experiment.

\subsection{Random-projector reference and scaler homogenization}
\label{app:randompenalty}
Write $J_P(\epsilon)=\epsilon^\top L_P\epsilon$ for~\eqref{eq:commutator}. Since $L_P\mathbf1=0$, adding a constant to every scale does not change the penalty. Under the penalty alone, $\dot\epsilon=-2\lambda L_P\epsilon$ preserves $\bar\epsilon$ and damps each positive-eigenvalue component of $L_P$. On a connected graph it converges to $\bar\epsilon\mathbf1$. This statement concerns ordinary gradient flow of this isolated quadratic, not AdamW with a task loss; neither shrinkage of the mean nor convergence of the trained model follows from it.

For a uniformly random rank-$k$ orthogonal projector $P$ in $\mathbb R^d$, and a fixed scaler vector independent of $P$,
\begin{equation}
\mathbb E_P[J_P(\epsilon)]
=\frac{k(d-k)}{(d-1)(d+2)}\|\epsilon-\bar\epsilon\mathbf1\|_2^2.
\label{eq:randompenalty}
\end{equation}
To verify the constant, write $P=QQ^\top$ for the first $k$ columns of a Haar orthogonal matrix. The spherical fourth moments give $\mathbb E[P_{ii}^2]=k(k+2)/(d(d+2))$. Since $\sum_{ij}P_{ij}^2=k$, symmetry gives $\mathbb E[P_{ij}^2]=k(d-k)/(d(d-1)(d+2))$ for $i\ne j$. Substitute into~\eqref{eq:commutator} and use $\sum_{i<j}(\epsilon_i-\epsilon_j)^2=d\|\epsilon-\bar\epsilon\mathbf1\|_2^2$.

This expectation motivates a centered-scaler control. It does not equate one fixed random projector with the pretrained projector: their positive-eigenvalue directions and strengths generally differ. It also cannot be substituted into training when the learned scalers depend on the sampled projector. A shared scalar nullspace alone does not imply identical trajectories or endpoints.

\subsection{Factor-scale identities}
\label{app:factorscale}
Write the inner matrix in~\eqref{eq:unified} as $K$. For any $a>0$, $(aE)K(D/a)=EKD$. Because each mixing operator is linear in its respective scaler, this transformation multiplies its two magnitudes by $a$ and $1/a$. For $S_L=0$, the additional identity
\begin{equation}
(aE)U_T(H/a^2)V_T^\top(aD)=E U_T H V_T^\top D
\end{equation}
shrinks both squared Frobenius penalties by $a^2$. It can make a raw factor penalty arbitrarily small with a fixed effective update if the core is allowed to diverge. This rescaling illustrates why the confinement bound assumes bounded factors. Measuring the effective update and the factor norms checks both sides of that statement.
\subsection{Magnitude flow in effective and factor coordinates}
\label{app:factorflow}
For a freely optimized effective update $x=\operatorname{vec}(\Delta)$, isolated gradient flow of $\beta\|x\|_2^2$ is radial, $\dot x=-2\beta x$, and preserves block fractions. If $x=f(\theta)$ is parameterized by adapter factors with Jacobian $J_\theta$, ordinary parameter gradient flow instead induces
\begin{equation}
\dot x=-2\beta J_\theta J_\theta^\top x.
\end{equation}
This need not be radial. A task objective or adaptive optimizer introduces further changes. For an explicit penalty-only example, fix $E=\diag(2,1)$, $D=V=I$, let $U$ rotate by $\pi/6$, fix $S_L=1$ and train only $H=h$. Then
\begin{equation}
C=\begin{bmatrix}7/4&-\sqrt3h/4\\-\sqrt3/4&5h/4\end{bmatrix},\quad
\|C\|_F^2=(13+7h^2)/4,\quad
p_\cross=\frac{3(1+h^2)}{4(13+7h^2)}.
\end{equation}
Magnitude-only flow decreases $h$ and changes $p_\cross$ ($0.075$ at $h=1$, approaching $3/52$ at zero). Thus orthogonal invariance of the norm objective does not force a constant trained cross share. On the MIX side, connected-projector, penalty-only homogenization predicts a scalar limit; finite task-trained cross shares and identity residuals remain empirical quantities.

\subsection{Initialization and residual leading structure}
\label{app:offset}
The fixed choice $S_L=I_k$ gives equal inner coefficients to the leading directions, independent of their pretrained singular values. Choosing $S_L=\Sigma_L$ would instead weight this contribution by the pretrained spectrum. All practical conditions use the identity choice; the experiment does not compare these two designs.

For the prescribed identity rotations, $E=D=s_0I$, $H=h_0I_r$, and $S_L=I_k$, insertion adds
\begin{equation}
\Delta_{\mathrm{init}}=s_0^2(U_LV_L^\top+h_0U_TV_T^\top),\quad
\fro{\Delta_{\mathrm{init}}}=s_0^2\sqrt{k+rh_0^2},\quad
p_{LL,\mathrm{init}}=\frac{k}{k+rh_0^2}.
\label{eq:initoffset}
\end{equation}
The cross blocks are initially zero. With $k=512,r=256$, the prescribed norm is approximately $0.22684$ for MRPC/STS-B/SST-2, $0.0022684$ for CoLA, and $0.0022628$ for RTE. Leading energy is approximately 99.50\% in the first four tasks and 99.995\% in RTE. These are analytical values from Table~\ref{tab:recipe}, not recovered checkpoint measurements. These recipe calculations alone do not quantify the offset relative to each archived trained update. The fresh confirmation export separately supplies measured initial and learned deltas.

For an attained leading block, define its identity alignment
\begin{equation}
\eta_I=\frac{(\operatorname{tr}C_{LL})^2}{k\fro{C_{LL}}^2},\qquad
0\le\eta_I\le1\quad(C_{LL}\ne0).
\label{eq:identityalignment}
\end{equation}
It equals the fraction of leading-block Frobenius energy in the projection onto $\operatorname{span}(I_k)$. Under constant scalers and $S_L=I_k$, $C_{LL}=abI_k$ has $\eta_I=1$ when $ab\ne0$, even though cross mixing vanishes. This motivates reporting $C-C_{\mathrm{init}}$, $C_{LL}-(\operatorname{tr}C_{LL}/k)I_k$, and centered scaler norms alongside total block energies. Subtracting the initialization and subtracting a fitted identity component answer different questions; neither can be reconstructed by subtracting squared norms. The fresh confirmation export supplies identity alignment and residual energies, analyzed in Appendix~\ref{app:reviewidentity}; centered scaler vectors remain unavailable. The older archive lacks these trained measurements.

\subsection{Metric definitions and aggregation}
For each adapted matrix, the reported metrics are
\begin{align}
\rho_F&=\fro{\Delta}/\fro{W_0},&
q_{\off}&=\fro{C_{\off}}/\fro{C},\\
\mathrm{Leak}_{LL}&=\op{C_{LL}}/\op{\Delta},&
C_{\off}&=\begin{bmatrix}C_{LL}&C_{LT}\\C_{TL}&0\end{bmatrix}.
\end{align}
The mixing magnitudes are operator norms, while their penalties use squared Frobenius norms. Leading left and right drift use the largest-principal-angle sine between the leading subspaces of $W_0$ and $W_0+\Delta$. The archived tables average each metric across the 48 modules within a run, then across seeds; they do not pool module energies before normalization.

The new fixed-endpoint study instead reports
\begin{equation}
\rho_{F,\mathrm{pool}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_\ell\|\Delta_\ell\|_F^2}{\sum_\ell\|W_{0,\ell}\|_F^2}},\qquad
p_{ab,\mathrm{pool}}=\frac{\sum_\ell\|C_{ab,\ell}\|_F^2}{\sum_\ell\|\Delta_\ell\|_F^2}.
\label{eq:pooledmetrics}
\end{equation}
Its equal-module comparison averages the individual $p_{ab,\ell}$ instead. A pooled fraction weights modules by update energy; an equal-module fraction does not. NORM's training objective averages squared module-relative norms, whereas calibration targets $\rho_{F,\mathrm{pool}}$. Neither matching a pooled norm nor rescaling it matches each module. Zero-update fractions are undefined. In both protocols, seeds, not modules, are the replication units.

\subsection{Executed fresh-protocol settings and input validation}
\label{app:freshrecipe}
The fresh study uses the pinned RoBERTa-base checkpoint \texttt{e2da8e2f811d1448a5b465c236feacd80ffbac7b} and GLUE dataset revision \texttt{bcdcba79d07bc864c1c254ccfcedcce55bcc9a8c}. A fixed 20\% inner-selection split is drawn from the training data with seed 271828; the official RTE and MRPC validation sets (277 and 408 examples) were held aside throughout training and selection, then evaluated once after the 18 checkpoints were frozen (Appendix~\ref{app:heldaside}). Inspecting tokenization parity on held-aside input text is not a task-score evaluation. No model or checkpoint was selected using those held-aside labels.

Executed manifests specify float32 training, microbatch 8 with four accumulation steps (effective batch 32), maximum sequence length 128, gradient-norm clipping at 1, and zero weight decay. AdamW uses separate adapter and task-head groups with linear learning-rate decay to zero and no warmup. The adapter rate is $10^{-2}$ on both tasks; head rates are $5\times10^{-4}$ on RTE and $10^{-3}$ on MRPC. Fixed endpoints are 5{,}670 and 2{,}760 optimizer steps, respectively. The practical adapter initializes both ambient scalers and tail coefficients at $0.01$ for RTE and $0.1$ for MRPC, with an identity leading block and no rotations. These initialization values belong to the practical study, not the strict-band branch. Task evaluation uses batches of 16; probe evaluation uses batches of 8. The secondary checkpoint maximizes inner-selection accuracy, excludes step 0, and breaks ties toward the earliest step. Candidates occur every 128 steps and at the registered fractions $\{0,.01,.05,.1,.25,.5,.75,1\}$ of the budget. The fixed endpoint remains primary regardless of the selected score.

Before calibration, a 32-example, 200-step overfit gate required training accuracy at least $0.95$; a 600-step unregularized learning gate required inner accuracy at least three points above the majority baseline. RTE passed at the adopted rates. On MRPC, the historical adapter rate $0.05$ reached the majority baseline ($0.6744$) and failed the second gate; the adopted common rate $0.01$ reached $0.8474$. This change preceded the comparisons and applied to every fresh MRPC arm. It does not revise the historical results.

The tokenizer repair explicitly restores the BPE model type omitted from its upstream serialization. An independent byte-level BPE implementation exactly reproduced input IDs and attention masks for 181 examples across both tasks and all prepared splits, including 51 truncated examples. Both durable invalidation records are enforced during extraction: all 39 runs from the corrupted-input campaigns are excluded. Run manifests bind source files, inputs, recipes and validation reports; an exported validation hash identifies the report, while independent reaggregation here audits the export rather than rerunning training.

The frozen-head probe contains 256 sampled examples from WikiText-2-raw-v1's test split~\citep{merity2017pointer}, corpus revision \texttt{b08601e04326c79dfdd32d625aee71d232d685c3}. Sampling seed 271828 and masking seed 161803 fix the examples and 15\% masking procedure. Summing per-example loss numerators and dividing by the 4{,}001 scored tokens gives original-backbone cross-entropy $2.095626$. This reference uses CPU float32; adapted probes use GPU float32. The corpus is held out from task adaptation, but is not guaranteed unseen during pretraining. The probe measures behavior under fixed examples and masks, not preserved knowledge in general.

\subsection{Power sensitivity under explicit variance assumptions}
\label{app:powersensitivity}
The nominal intervals describe uncertainty; their half-widths are not minimum detectable effects. To make the small-seed limitation quantitative, consider a hypothetical two-sided paired $t$ test with $n=3$, independent Gaussian seed differences, significance level $0.05$, and fixed true difference SD $\sigma$. Its power at a mean difference $\delta$ is
\begin{equation}
\Pr\!\left\{|T_{2,\sqrt3\delta/\sigma}|>t_{0.975,2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here $T_{\nu,\lambda}$ is noncentral Student $t$. Solving for 80\% power gives $|\delta|\simeq3.26\sigma$. Table~\ref{tab:powersensitivity} substitutes each observed paired sample SD as an uncertain variance scenario, including all task-score, task-NLL and probe contrasts. These are post hoc design sensitivities, not achieved power, effects excluded by the experiment, or an equivalence margin. At three seeds the variance itself is poorly estimated; all entries scale linearly with the assumed $\sigma$ and condition on the fixed evaluation examples.

\subsection{Strictly confined adaptation: complete RTE results}
\label{app:bandstudy}

\paragraph{Training and evaluation protocol.} We use the pretrained RoBERTa-base encoder and a newly initialized two-layer classification head that maps its sentence-pair representation to two label scores. Softmax converts these scores to probabilities. All conditions train this head; only adapter conditions also train coefficients and, where enabled, rotations. The original encoder weights remain frozen. A fixed 20\% split of RTE's training set, drawn with seed 271828, supplies the inner-selection examples; training uses the remaining 80\%. Conditions share the head initialization and data order within each seed. All train for 5{,}670 AdamW steps with effective batch size 32, adapter learning rate $10^{-2}$, head learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, linear decay, no warmup, no weight decay, and no interaction penalty. Other input and optimizer settings follow Appendix~\ref{app:freshrecipe}; the zero-update initialization below replaces the practical adapter's initialization. We report accuracy and cross-entropy on the inner-selection split at the fixed final step, not the best-scoring checkpoint. The official RTE validation set has not been evaluated for this study.

The separate RTE study crosses three 256-direction bands with diagonal coefficients or rotations in the last 64 directions, plus an original-backbone head-only reference. Leading, middle and tail bands are $[0,256)$, $[256,512)$ and $[512,768)$. All 48 attention updates use $\Delta=U_BHV_B^\top$, start at zero, and omit the ambient scalers and leading-identity contribution. Three seeds (17, 42, 123) give 21 registered confirmations. Two seed-31415 timing pilots are recorded separately. Off-band energy is structurally zero, with a $10^{-8}$ float32 layer-test bound.

Each rotation uses PyTorch 2.5.1's \texttt{nn.utils.parametrizations.orthogonal} with \texttt{orthogonal\_map="matrix\_exp"} and the default \texttt{use\_trivialization=True}. Its square weight is initialized to the identity before registration. The matrix exponential maps a skew-symmetric parameter matrix to an orthogonal output throughout optimization. Each pair stores $2q^2$ trainable entries, while its two orthogonal outputs have $q(q-1)$ combined degrees of freedom (Appendix~\ref{app:cost}).

The final export contains all 21 confirmation outcomes and the two timing pilots. Table~\ref{tab:bandcomplete} summarizes every arm; Table~\ref{tab:bandpaired} reports all 18 registered paired contrasts; Table~\ref{tab:bandavailable} gives every individual endpoint. All scores are inner-selection results. The freeze ledger joins all 21 fixed checkpoints to their validation hashes and the same registered protocol. Although repository revisions differ across launches, their experiment-source trees are identical; the supplement verifies all 46 recorded source-file fingerprints against a saved source snapshot. Within-band off-diagonal energy is computed directly from compact cores, with signed floating-point residuals retained in the raw export for diagonal arms.

\subsection{Reconstructing four-block allocation}
\label{app:blockallocation}
The 27 run summaries are accompanied by 1,296 module-level records: 48 adapted matrices for each A/B/C run. Their saved Frobenius leakage ratios use $\|\Delta\|_F$ in the denominator, whereas the off-tail ratio uses $\|C\|_F$. Write these recorded quantities as $\ell_{ab}$ for $ab\in\{LL,LT,TL\}$ and $q$ for the off-tail ratio. To obtain an allocation normalized in the recorded coordinate frame, we compute per matrix
\begin{equation}
\widehat p_{ab}=q^2\frac{\ell_{ab}^2}{\ell_{LL}^2+\ell_{LT}^2+\ell_{TL}^2},\qquad
\widehat p_{TT}=1-q^2.
\label{eq:recordedallocation}
\end{equation}
For exact orthogonal bases these agree with the fractions defined in Section~\ref{sec:measurement}. With saved finite-precision bases, the inferred discrepancy $\left|(\ell_{LL}^2+\ell_{LT}^2+\ell_{TL}^2)/q^2-1\right|$ is at most $2.64\cdot10^{-4}$ across the archive. The normalization makes the four recorded fractions sum to one without assuming perfect numerical orthogonality. Diagnostic denominators also include a $10^{-12}$ numerical stabilizer. We square each matrix's ratios before averaging, never a task-mean ratio.

\subsection{A reusable spectral report}
\label{app:reportcard}
Use a fixed pretrained checkpoint, adapted module list, and declared cutoff for all methods. The primary cutoff here follows the adapter tail dimension, not a measured spectral gap. For each saved effective matrix, record both $\Delta_{\mathrm{total}}=W_{\mathrm{eff}}-W_0$ and $\Delta_{\mathrm{learned}}=W_{\mathrm{eff}}-W_{\mathrm{eff}}(0)$; report zero-update fractions as undefined. The report comprises absolute Frobenius block energies, their fractions, $\rho_F$, operator norm, stable rank, and the dimension-only fractions. Add leading identity alignment $\eta_I$ and absolute non-identity residual energy under both delta definitions. Export each module's norm and absolute block energies, the cumulative energy curve, and threshold-count curves; a cumulative energy curve alone cannot recover relative-norm thresholds. We report $10^{-6}$ for small-norm counts and both $10^{-6},10^{-3}$ for sensitivity subsets. Future comparisons must declare thresholds before inspection and retain the underlying values. Use the aggregation specified by each table: the focused headline pools squared energies, while equal-module summaries normalize before averaging. This report is diagnostic, not a preservation pass/fail criterion.

Let $P,P'$ be rank-$k$ left projectors before and after adaptation, and $m=\min(k,d-k)$. Supplement the largest-angle sine with
\begin{equation}
d_{\mathrm{ch}}=\frac{\fro{P-P'}}{\sqrt{2m}},\qquad
o=\frac{\operatorname{tr}(PP')}{k},\qquad
d_{\mathrm{ch}}^2=\frac{k}{m}(1-o).
\label{eq:chordal}
\end{equation}
Here $d_{\mathrm{ch}}^2$ averages squared sines over the $m$ potentially nonzero angles, including any zeros within them; $o$ is normalized overlap, with random-independent-subspace expectation $k/d$. Apply the same definitions on the right. Report the boundary values $\sigma_k,\sigma_{k+1}$, their gap, and a prespecified cutoff sweep, such as $k\in\{16,64,128,256,512\}$. Singular-subspace perturbation bounds require separation conditions~\citep{wedin1972perturbation}; a norm/gap quotient is not an unconditional prediction of drift. A boundary tie is flagged, not presented as uniquely defined ranked stability.

Pair these measurements with validation and untouched-test performance, and one frozen-pretraining-head behavior probe on the same checkpoints when retention is studied. Store per-seed values at both a fixed training step and the selected checkpoint; log diagnostics separately from optimization costs. Full ranked-subspace diagnostics require dense SVDs, $O(d^3)$ for square matrices; a truncated or randomized approximation must report its rank, residual tolerance and convergence checks, particularly near cutoff ties. The archived study supplies total-delta allocation and maximum-angle drift, but not the full initialization-corrected or chordal report. Those fields require checkpoint evaluation, not imputation from the current ratios.

\section{Computational cost of the spectral instruments}
\label{app:cost}
We distinguish trainable capacity from frozen storage, setup cost, and the computation required to apply the adapter. Let a square layer have width $d$, selected tail dimension $r$, and rotated sub-block dimension $q\le r$. Let $b$ count the token vectors processed by that layer in one forward call. All costs below are per adapted matrix and exclude the base model's own storage and forward computation, the task head, and hardware-dependent constants.

\paragraph{SVD initialization and frozen storage.}
A dense SVD of a $d\times d$ matrix has conventional $O(d^3)$ arithmetic cost. For an $m\times n$ matrix with $p=\min(m,n)$, the corresponding economy-SVD cost is $O(mnp)$ and its two bases contain $(m+n)p$ entries. This is a one-time setup cost per distinct weight decomposition, not a decomposition at every optimization step. The practical implementation computes the SVD in float32 before casting stored factors to the adapter dtype, so initialization workspace can exceed persistent basis storage.

A tail-only implementation needs $2dr$ basis entries to apply its update. The implemented leading-plus-tail construction retains the complete bases, totaling $2d^2$ entries, plus $O(d)$ fixed coefficients and any orthogonal-map state. Freezing these entries avoids gradients and optimizer moments for them; it does not remove their memory cost. Setting the leading coefficients to zero in a full-basis implementation does not itself discard those bases or shorten its matrix products. Diagnostics that retain an additional full reference must also be included in memory accounting.

\paragraph{Trainable entries versus effective degrees of freedom.}
Fixed-tail coefficients require $r$ trainable entries. Two dense $q\times q$ rotation parameters add $2q^2$ stored trainable entries; their orthogonal outputs have only $q(q-1)$ combined manifold degrees of freedom. Ambient input/output scalers add $2d$ entries. Thus the practical unrotated and partially rotated forms store $r+2d$ and $r+2d+2q^2$ trainable entries, respectively, before the task head. A directly trained unrestricted tail core instead has $r^2$ entries. These counts describe different parameterizations, not equal-capacity interventions. For example, $d=768,r=256$ gives 1,792 trainable entries per unrotated coordinate-scaled matrix but 1,179,648 frozen basis entries. The GLUE score comparison is not used to infer total training-memory savings.

\paragraph{Forward computation.}
Applying a diagonal tail update through its thin factors costs $O(bdr)$; ambient scaling adds only $O(bd)$. If partial rotations are materialized into the basis first, the two basis products add $O(dq^2)$ per reconstruction, in addition to the orthogonal-map construction cost. Dense $q\times q$ maps can incur $O(q^3)$ arithmetic. A dense tail core applied between thin basis products instead costs $O(bdr+br^2)$.

The practical forward path applies leading and tail basis products separately; together they span the complete bases. Its adapter branch therefore costs $O(bd^2)$ even when $q=0$, plus rotation construction when enabled. This is additional to the $O(bd^2)$ base projection. A rank-$\ell$ LoRA branch costs $O(bd\ell)$, so a smaller trainable count need not mean a cheaper forward pass. Backpropagation also differentiates through the adapter operations, and end-to-end peak memory depends on retained activations, dtype, optimizer state, and checkpointing. The arithmetic counts alone do not establish a measured throughput ratio.

At two bytes per entry, the full frozen bases cost 2.25, 4, and 64 MiB per matrix at widths $d=768,1024,4096$, respectively. Each is twice the storage of the corresponding base projection, and the two basis products add approximately twice its matrix-product arithmetic before rotations or penalties. Across the 48 RoBERTa-base projections, 56.6M basis entries are about 45\% of a 125M-parameter backbone's weight storage at equal dtype; the fresh float32 runs require twice the two-byte figures. No measured usable-width limit is established beyond the tested backbones. At larger widths this quadratic storage and cubic SVD setup favor thin selected-band implementations where the update family permits them, or measuring conventional adapters after training. A strict tail-only branch at $r=d/3$ reduces basis storage by threefold; zeroing a leading core in the existing full-basis path does not realize that saving.

\paragraph{Regularization and diagnostics.}
Directly forming $M_E=U_L^\top E U_T$ and $M_D=V_L^\top D V_T$ costs $O(dkr)$ for a leading dimension $k=d-r$; evaluating their squared Frobenius penalties then costs $O(kr)$. The fresh runs instead cache the two leading projectors and use the equivalent commutator formula~\eqref{eq:commutator}. This requires $O(d^2k)$ setup and $O(d^2)$ extra storage per layer, followed by $O(d^2)$ work per step. Raw mixing is measured in every arm; gradients through it are computed only when it is penalized. Neither form requires an SVD of the adapted weight. By contrast, exact ranked-subspace diagnostics use a new dense SVD of $W_0+\Delta$ and can cost $O(d^3)$ per measured layer/checkpoint. Diagnostic time should be reported separately from optimization time and should not be charged only to the spectral methods in a controlled comparison.

\paragraph{Merging and deployment.}
At evaluation with dropout disabled, the additive update can be materialized and merged into $W_0$. A thin diagonal-tail construction costs $O(d^2r)$ to materialize, whereas the current full-basis product costs $O(d^3)$, plus the rotation and scaler operations; dense materialization requires $O(d^2)$ output storage. After merging and removing the adapter branch, the linear layer has the base model's shape and inference-time matrix multiplication. This removes the extra branch at deployment, not the preceding SVD, training, or merge costs. In multi-adapter serving, frozen pretrained bases can be shared across tasks; their quadratic storage need not be duplicated per adapter. Keeping branches permits task switching with additional arithmetic, whereas caching separately merged weights requires a dense weight copy per task. The 192-fold branch-arithmetic comparison is not a 192-fold trainable-adapter storage ratio.
